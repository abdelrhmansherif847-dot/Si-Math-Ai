# EST I Math — Distractor Model

**Artifact 4 of 8.** Evidence tiers: [`01-est-exam-dna.md` §0](01-est-exam-dna.md).

---

## 1. The governing finding

Every one of the 600 wrong options across the corpus that we could account for
traces to a **specific, nameable thing a student did** — not to a random plausible
number.

We classified each item by the dominant error its distractor set encodes. All 200
items resolved into nine classes (one item excluded: its coefficient could not be
read reliably from the scan). **All nine classes appear in all four forms.**

| Class | Name | n | % | Per form (1/2/3/5) | Forms |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| **D1** | Unfinished step — the value from stopping early | 40 | 20.1% | 8 / 9 / 16 / 7 | 4/4 |
| **D2** | Concept confusion — a neighbouring idea applied | 34 | 17.1% | 10 / 11 / 3 / 10 | 4/4 |
| **D3** | Sign or orientation | 31 | 15.6% | 8 / 9 / 6 / 8 | 4/4 |
| **D4** | Structural / wrong form of a correct answer | 20 | 10.1% | 7 / 7 / 4 / 2 | 4/4 |
| **D5** | Wrong member of the right set | 20 | 10.1% | 2 / 3 / 5 / 10 | 4/4 |
| **D6** | Wrong base, denominator or scale | 19 | 9.5% | 4 / 3 / 9 / 3 | 4/4 |
| **D7** | Computation slip on a correct method | 17 | 8.5% | 5 / 3 / 2 / 7 | 4/4 |
| **D8** | Boundary / strictness | 10 | 5.0% | 2 / 3 / 3 / 2 | 4/4 |
| **D9** | Off-by-one in a count of periods or intervals | 8 | 4.0% | 3 / 2 / 2 / 1 | 4/4 |

**T2 — the taxonomy is stable across forms.** Nine classes, nine appearing
everywhere, with per-form counts that move but never vanish. The *ordering* moves
too (Form 3 leans heavily on D1 and D6; Form 5 leans on D5 and D7), so the class
*shares* are T3 and the class *presence* is T2.

**Generation rule (T2):** every generated form must exercise all nine classes,
and no class may exceed 30% of the form.

## 2. The classes

### D1 — Unfinished step · 20.1% · the largest class

**The distractor is the value the student holds one line before the end.**

This class is the direct partner of the derived-target lever (artifact 3, L2).
When the stem asks for `k+4`, the option `k` is on the page. When it asks for the
diameter, the radius is on the page. When it asks for half the greater root, the
greater root is there — and so is half the lesser, and the lesser.

Sub-shapes and their frequencies:

| Sub-shape | n | Forms |
|---|---:|---|
| The un-derived value (asked `f(x)`, offered the intermediate) | 8 | 3/4 |
| One contributing term found, the rest not | 7 | 4/4 |
| A partial scan of a chart or list | 5 | 3/4 |
| One branch of a case analysis resolved | 4 | 3/4 |
| A fixed cost or constant omitted from the model | 4 | 3/4 |
| The sum reported where the mean was asked, or vice versa | 3 | 3/4 |

**T2 — this is the class most responsible for the exam's character.** Its
presence means a student cannot self-check by "my answer is on the list": their
half-answer is on the list too.

**Authoring rule:** if an item has a derived target, the un-derived value is
**mandatory** as one of the three distractors. Artifact 7 gates it.

### D2 — Concept confusion · 17.1%

**The student applied a genuinely different but adjacent concept.**

Not a slip — a wrong idea, correctly executed. The corpus's catalogue:

| Confusion | Where it appears |
|---|---|
| Domain offered when range is asked | rational functions |
| Reciprocal offered when the inverse is asked | function inverses |
| Permutations offered when combinations are asked (and the reverse) | counting |
| With-replacement offered when without is stipulated | probability |
| Slope zero offered when the slope is undefined | vertical lines |
| A different measure of centre | statistics |
| Circumference formula with radius/diameter interchanged | circles |
| The slant height used as the perpendicular height | cones |
| The diagonal squared offered as the area | squares |
| The quartile boundary offered as the interquartile range | statistics |
| Percentage change offered as percentage-point change | data reading |
| A count offered where a percentage is asked, and the reverse | data reading |

**T2 — the corpus builds 2×2 grids over paired confusions.** Where two binary
confusions apply at once, all four cells are on the page: with/without
replacement crossed with ordered/unordered; increase/decrease crossed with two
base choices; parallel/perpendicular slope crossed with two intercepts.

**Authoring rule:** for any item whose topic has a canonical near-neighbour
concept, the near-neighbour's answer is a required distractor.

### D3 — Sign or orientation · 15.6%

**T2 — the corpus's most mechanical class, and it is nearly always a full grid.**
Where an item's answer has two independent sign-bearing components, all four
combinations appear as the four options: `(±a, ±b)`, `±(x ± c)²`, both signs on
each of two roots, `±p/q` with two magnitudes.

Also in this class: transposed coordinates `(a,b)` versus `(b,a)`; cross-paired
coordinates that take the *x* of one intersection and the *y* of the other; an
inverted ratio.

**T2 — a full sign grid means three of the four options are indistinguishable by
estimation.** This is why sign items are hard despite being one-step: nothing can
be eliminated by magnitude.

**Authoring rule:** when the answer carries two independent signs or two
independent magnitude choices, use the complete 2×2. **Do not** half-fill it with
one grid cell and two unrelated values — the corpus never does.

### D4 — Structural / wrong form · 10.1%

**The distractor is not a number at all** — it is a *structurally* wrong
expression, model or classification.

This is the entire distractor set for modelling items (artifact 2, A02): the
operation reversed, the factor not distributed, the term not doubled, a monthly
rate multiplied by days it should not touch. It also covers wrong subsets in
Roman-numeral items, and the structurally wrong rearrangement of a literal
formula (`√(Bσ/ρg)` versus `Bσ/ρg` versus `√(Bρ/σg)`).

**T2 — a modelling item's distractors never contain arithmetic errors**, because
there is no arithmetic to get wrong. Any generated modelling item whose
distractors are numbers is out of register.

### D5 — Wrong member of the right set · 10.1%

**The student did everything right and reported the wrong element.**

Distinct from D1: the work is *complete*, not partial. The greater root when the
smaller is asked; the abscissa when the ordinate is asked; the middle term of a
ratio when the largest is asked; the width when the length is asked; the row
above the one the ordinal reference names.

**T2 — where a "which member" selection exists, the other member is always
present.** Four items across three forms give two roots and ask for one; the
other is an option every time.

### D6 — Wrong base, denominator or scale · 9.5%

**The right numerator over the wrong denominator, or the right number at the
wrong scale.**

The purest example is the two-way table item whose *entire* distractor set is the
same numerator over three different plausible denominators — the row total, the
column total, the grand total. Also: percentage change computed against the new
value rather than the old; a graph read without applying the axis's stated scale
factor; a reverse percentage computed by adding the percentage back to the final
value rather than dividing.

**T2 — this class is why "read the stem's denominator" is a real EST skill.**

### D7 — Computation slip on a correct method · 8.5%

The smallest legitimate class and the one to use most sparingly. A single
arithmetic or algebraic slip on an otherwise correct method.

**T2 — the corpus uses it as a filler, not as a design.** It is concentrated in
one-step items where there is no richer error available (Form 5, which has the
most single-step items, carries the most of this class at 7).

**Authoring rule:** at most one purely-slip distractor per item, and never all
three. An item whose three distractors are three arithmetic slips is the clearest
signature of a synthetic question.

### D8 — Boundary / strictness · 5.0%

The excluded endpoint; the point exactly on the circle when *inside* is asked;
the inclusive reading of a range when the exclusive is meant. Paired with
artifact 3's L5.

**T2 — 10 items, all four forms.** The distractor is arithmetically correct and
conceptually wrong, which makes it the hardest kind to eliminate.

### D9 — Off-by-one · 4.0%

One period too many or too few in a growth calculation; one year too many in a
day count; the interval count `n` where `n−1` is right in a rate of change;
`1.01¹⁰` and `1.01¹²` flanking the correct `1.01¹¹`.

**T2 — every growth or extrapolation item in the corpus carries an off-by-one
distractor.** It is effectively mandatory for that family.

## 3. Structural rules for the option set

**T2 — options are ordered by magnitude, not by plausibility.** Numeric options
run ascending in the overwhelming majority of items; where they are not
orderable (expressions, coordinates, categorical labels) they follow the
structure of the object — sign grids run `(−,−) (−,+) (+,−) (+,+)`; Roman-numeral
subsets run from smallest to largest. **The key letter is therefore determined by
the answer's magnitude, not chosen** — which is the direct cause of the key
statistics in artifact 1 §8 and of the fact that they look random.

**T2 — there are no throwaway options.** We found no option in the corpus that a
student with partial understanding could dismiss without doing mathematics. This
is the highest bar an authored item must clear.

**T2 — option spacing is deliberately tight where it matters.** Boundary items
differ by one integer; sign grids differ only in sign; denominator items share a
numerator. Options are far apart only when the difficulty is elsewhere (a
scaled-axis item's options differ by a factor of ten because the trap is the
scale).

**T3 — option *format* is uniform inside an item.** All four are fractions, or
all four are decimals to the same precision, or all four are coordinate pairs. We
found one deliberate exception, where a single option carries its own domain
caveat, which functions as a hint that the option is the special one. Uniformity
is otherwise absolute, and a mixed-format option set reads as synthetic.

**T2 — "None of the above" exists but is rare.** Two items across two forms, and
in both it is the *incorrect* option. Permissible in roughly half of generated
forms, at most once, and **never as the key** — using it as the key on rare
evidence would be inventing a pattern.

**T2 — options may be objects, not values.** Four graphs; four number lines; four
systems of equations; four rows of a comparison table; four named students; four
prose claims. Four items across three forms. Any delivery surface for a generated
EST form must render these.

## 4. What a synthetic item looks like — the checklist this artifact exists to prevent

From reading 200 authentic distractor sets, these are the tells:

1. **Three arithmetic slips.** The commonest failure. If all three distractors are
   "the answer ± a small error", the item is not an EST item.
2. **A throwaway option.** One choice that is obviously absurd — an order of
   magnitude out, or a negative where only positives are possible.
3. **The un-derived value missing.** A derived-target item whose intermediate is
   *not* on the page inverts the exam's whole design.
4. **A half-filled sign grid.** Two cells of a 2×2 plus two unrelated numbers.
5. **Mixed option formats.** Two fractions, a decimal and a surd.
6. **Numeric distractors on a modelling item.**
7. **Options not in magnitude order** when they are orderable.
8. **A distractor nobody would produce.** Every wrong option must be traceable to
   a sentence beginning "a student who…". If that sentence cannot be written, the
   option is filler.

Artifact 7 turns items 1–8 into per-item gates. Item 8 is the one that catches the
rest, and it is the one an author is most tempted to skip.

## 5. The authoring procedure

For each item, in this order:

1. **Solve it.** Get the key.
2. **Identify the derived target**, if the item has one. If it does, **the
   un-derived value is distractor 1** (D1). Non-negotiable.
3. **Name the near-neighbour concept.** If the topic has one, its answer is
   distractor 2 (D2).
4. **Check for an independent sign or magnitude choice.** If two exist, fill the
   full 2×2 (D3) and stop — the grid is the option set.
5. **Otherwise fill remaining slots** from D4–D6 and D8–D9 as the item's structure
   allows, preferring the class that the *item's own topic* makes available: a
   growth item takes D9; a proportion item takes D6; an inequality takes D8.
6. **At most one D7 slip**, and only if steps 2–5 could not fill the set.
7. **Write the sentence** for each distractor: "a student who ______ gets this."
   Any option without such a sentence is replaced, not kept.
8. **Order by magnitude** and read off the key letter. **Do not choose the key.**
9. **Check the form-level balance afterwards**, per artifact 8 — never by
   reordering an item whose order carries meaning.

Step 9's exception matters: where options are structurally ordered (a sign grid, a
Roman-numeral subset ladder, an ascending numeric series), **reordering to fix a
key imbalance destroys the design**. Fix the balance by choosing a different
number for the item, not by shuffling its options.
